# Drafting from research materials

Determine the passage's job, audience, and available evidence. Use the supplied
notes, results, manuscript, and conversation before asking for additional context.
Do not require a venue, a fixed number of contributions, or a new workspace merely
to start writing. If an essential fact is missing, ask a focused question or state
the limitation while drafting supported material.

For a substantial section or paper, identify the central question, conceptual
contribution, and most important supported conclusion. Use these to form a compact
argument outline connecting reasoning, evidence, and limits. Tie substantive
claims to the actual provided source. This may remain working context; do not
create an administrative evidence ledger for a small task. Preserve the difference
between established findings, interpretations, proposals, and open questions.

Organize the narrative around what the work helps readers understand, rather than
a sequence of technical steps or a tour of source files. Explain why the question
matters using the supplied research motivation; do not substitute a minor technical
gap for a supported substantive question. State the contribution concretely without
inflating or understating it. A broader proposed direction can frame a restricted
demonstration if their different evidentiary status is clear. Adapt the organization
to the work: theoretical, methodological, and empirical papers need different
exposition, and an existing manuscript may already have a sound structure.

Use supplied, verified references for literature synthesis. Compare sources along
the dimensions relevant to the argument instead of summarizing one paper per
sentence. Do not invent a bibliographic record, claim a paper establishes something
without adequate source support, or turn drafting into an unrequested literature
search. Identify missing citation support needed for the requested passage
separately.

Read the actual result sources before writing quantitative claims. Include the
metric, comparison, setting, uncertainty, and qualifications that determine their
meaning. Do not fill missing results with plausible values or convert an intended
experiment into a completed one. When positioning preliminary work, retain the
status of relevant planned evaluations in a concise statement; describing a
prototype alone may leave its evaluation status unclear. Missing evidence need
not become a list of caveats when the drafted claim is already precisely scoped.
If the user wants an incomplete draft, mark any
placeholders explicitly so they cannot be mistaken for findings.

Before writing a substantial section, sketch the reader path: one line per
paragraph giving its role and how it follows from the one before. The
[review](review.md) compares the draft's reverse outline with this sketch. Keep the
outline, sketch, and checks in working context and deliver the requested prose.
Apply the natural-prose checks while drafting, not only in revision: write the
method and findings, not the process of checking them (U5, E1–E9). For a full
manuscript, write the supported sections and explain material omissions. Apply the
core delivery rule (U6), and stop rather than appending advice about further work.

Check terminology and claims across the sections you changed (A10). Preserve the
user's file organization and output format; use a managed Quill workspace only when
it is already in use or requested.
